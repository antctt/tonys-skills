# Irresistible hooks

Create hooks that earn attention by opening a curiosity loop and then paying it off with real value. Do not substitute a list of generic "viral hook" templates for reasoning about the viewer, topic, and payoff.

## Before writing

Infer as much as possible from the user's prompt before asking questions. Identify:

- the target viewer
- the topic and format
- the viewer's likely benefit, pain, or existing interest
- the strongest true payoff the content can actually deliver
- any available visual proof, demo moment, object, screen, result, or motion

If details are missing but a useful hook can still be written, make conservative assumptions and proceed. Do not invent statistics, outcomes, credentials, or claims just to make the hook stronger.

## Build the hook

### 1. Find the payoff first

Start with the most compelling true idea, result, reveal, or proof in the content. The hook should point toward that payoff. If the body cannot deliver the promise, weaken the hook rather than overpromise.

### 2. Write the context lean

Use the first one or two short sentences to do two jobs:

1. Make the topic immediately clear so the right viewer can self-select in.
2. Give that viewer a reason to lean in.

Create the lean with one of these when it fits:

- common ground the viewer already understands
- a concrete benefit they want
- a pain point they want solved
- a simple metaphor for a difficult idea
- a genuinely surprising fact or visual

Prefer the viewer's desired outcome over technical background. For a niche topic, wrap the unknown idea in something familiar before introducing jargon.

### 3. Add the scroll-stop interjection

Insert one short line that interrupts the direction established by the context lean. Contrast words such as "but", "yet", "however", or "although" often work because they signal that the obvious path is about to change.

The line should create tension, not empty drama. It is a setup for the next sentence.

### 4. Deliver the contrarian snapback

Move in an unexpected direction while staying on the same topic. The snapback should make the viewer revise what they thought the video was about.

A strong snapback is:

- specific
- relevant to the original topic
- meaningfully different from the initial lean
- supported by the content that follows

The contrast can be large or small. Do not force a dramatic contrarian claim when the material only supports a modest surprise.

### 5. Compress speed to value

Give the viewer a useful insight, proof point, example, or reveal in the hook or immediately after it. Do not bury the best material deep in the script.

For short-form video, treat the opening seconds as scarce. For longer YouTube content, still prove value early rather than spending the opening on setup. Use these as pacing heuristics, not fixed platform rules.

### 6. Layer the visual hook

When the output is video, pair the spoken hook with:

- 3 to 5 words of large, clear on-screen text that establishes context
- the most compelling visual available
- enough motion to attract attention without making the message hard to parse

The spoken line, title text, and visual should reinforce the same idea rather than compete for attention.

### 7. Tighten the opening rhythm

Make the first sentences staccato: short, clear, and dense with meaning. Short sentences force clarity when attention is most fragile. Let sentence length expand later once the viewer is oriented.

## Use common ground deliberately

For complex or unfamiliar topics, use a known brand, person, cultural reference, category, or familiar situation as a bridge. The source framework calls this "cult hopping".

Use it only when the reference makes the idea easier to understand. Do not add celebrities or brands merely for borrowed attention.

## Output behavior

Follow the user's requested format and number of options. If they do not specify a format, return 3 meaningfully different hook options.

For each video hook, default to:

1. **Spoken hook**: the actual opening lines.
2. **On-screen text**: 3 to 5 words.
3. **Opening visual**: what the viewer sees while the hook lands.
4. **First value beat**: the proof, reveal, or useful idea shown immediately after the hook.

Keep explanations brief unless the user asks for analysis.

When critiquing an existing hook, diagnose the weak stage first. Check topic clarity, lean, interruption, snapback, value latency, visual support, and sentence density. Then rewrite it.

When writing a full script, use this skill to design the opening and the transition into the body. Do not keep repeating hook mechanics after the viewer already understands the premise.

## Quality check

Before returning a hook, verify:

- The right viewer can tell what the content is about immediately.
- The hook connects to a benefit, pain, interest, or familiar frame.
- The interjection changes direction rather than adding filler.
- The snapback creates a real information gap.
- The content can deliver the promised payoff.
- Useful value arrives early.
- The first sentences are short enough to process quickly.
- Video outputs have aligned spoken, text, and visual hooks.
- Any cultural reference makes the idea clearer rather than noisier.
